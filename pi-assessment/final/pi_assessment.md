# PI Assessment: Gene mutant dosage (INCOMMON) for our glioma research

My position is to pursue with modifications. Asking how many copies of a gene carry a mutation, not only whether it is present, is worth testing in our data, but adopting INCOMMON for our glioma work requires a defined, narrower use case.

The methodological starting point is our 2017 tumor-only caller, LumosVar, which already combined allele-frequency mixture modeling with allelic copy number, germline states and subclonal fractions. INCOMMON shares this mixture logic but assumes clonal mutations and allows mutant multiplicity to vary independently of major-allele copy number. Its advances are whole-genome-derived priors over total copy number (k) and mutant copies (m), which allow inference from a handful of panel mutations without a matched normal or segmentation, and associations across 60,000 clinical samples. Its clinical summary, FAM, is the posterior mean of m/k; 1 of 2 and 4 of 8 mutant copies both give 0.5.

However, the paper reports no glioma-specific result. Key glioma dosage events are focal oncogene amplifications, often on ecDNA. INCOMMON does not model wild-type amplification, caps k at 8 and assumes every mutation and copy-number state is clonal. In Webb et al., bulk DNA coverage put one glioblastoma at over 30 EGFR and about 15 MDM2 copies. In a TP53-mutant glioblastoma, an EGFR-high subclone had lost heterozygosity of chromosome 17, which carries TP53, so the TP53 allele configuration was not uniform across the tumor. A single clonal estimate cannot resolve that regional heterogeneity.

A pilot can build on our published glioma cohort, which included tumor/normal exomes, bulk RNA and spatial transcriptomics. After confirming access, sample matching and eligible mutations, first run INCOMMON on exomes restricted to panel genes; compare k with full-data copy-number estimates and m with mutation-specific reference estimates integrating variant reads, purity and allele-specific copy number, reporting uncertainty and prior sensitivity. This tests inference, not panel chemistry; the published FACETS comparison supports panel feasibility but does not validate mutant multiplicity. If calibration passes, then test whether m or FAM improves held-out prediction of target-gene or pathway expression beyond mutation status, total copy number, purity and cell composition, with patients, not spots, as the unit of replication. The paper's survival-derived cutoffs should not be reused.

Spatial work must respect scale. A sample-level estimate cannot be assigned to spots, RNA allele fractions are not DNA truth, and the probe-based FFPE spatial assay used for our validation cohort cannot measure expressed-SNP LOH. GMD is also not ecDNA dosage: our ecDNA work uses regional FISH-based dose proxies and aims to recover segregation-derived variation through local contrasts and structural adjustment; FAM summarizes a mutation under a clonal model.

I therefore recommend running calibration first and the added-value test only if calibration passes, restricting use to clonal, mutation-bearing loci within the model's range, and keeping independent structural evidence for ecDNA.
